# Letter Writing Etiquette: Dos and Don'ts

Good letter writing etiquette is what separates a note that reads as thoughtful and professional from one that quietly undermines you. The rules aren't complicated, but they matter — a misjudged greeting or a sloppy sign-off can distract from an otherwise strong message. Whether you're writing to a colleague, a client, a friend, or a potential employer, here are the dos and don'ts that keep your correspondence polished.

## The Dos

### Do open with the right salutation

Match the greeting to the reader. Use "Dear Mr. Clarke," or "Dear Ms. Rivera," for formal letters (in the US, a colon after the name is common in business writing). When you don't know the name, "Dear Sir or Madam," works in the UK and "To Whom It May Concern," in the US. For friends, a simple "Dear Sarah," or "Hi Tom," is fine.

### Do keep your language clear and courteous

Say what you mean in plain words, and stay polite even when the subject is difficult — a complaint or a refusal lands better when the tone stays respectful. Avoid jargon, sarcasm, and anything that reads as passive-aggressive.

### Do follow standard formatting

A formal letter should include the date, the recipient's details, a proper greeting, well-spaced paragraphs, and a matching sign-off. One-inch margins and a readable font keep it looking professional. For a full walkthrough of the layout, see our guide on [how to write a letter](/en/blog/write-perfect-letter).

### Do proofread before you send

Typos and grammar slips chip away at your credibility. Read the letter twice — once for sense, once purely for errors — and, for anything important, ask someone else to look it over.

### Do include a way to reach you

In formal correspondence, add your contact details so the recipient can respond easily. Close with a polite line thanking them for their time.

## The Don'ts

### Don't be too casual in a formal letter

Slang, abbreviations, and emojis have no place in professional correspondence. Save the relaxed tone for personal letters and keep formal writing measured and respectful.

### Don't ignore the sign-off conventions

The closing should mirror your greeting. In the UK, use "Yours sincerely," when you opened with the recipient's name and "Yours faithfully," when you didn't. In the US, "Sincerely," or "Best regards," cover most situations. Mixing these up is a classic etiquette slip.

### Don't rush it

A hurried letter reads as careless. Take a moment to consider the reader's perspective and the purpose of your message. If it's a sensitive matter, drafting it and returning later almost always improves the result.

### Don't forget the envelope

Etiquette doesn't stop at the last line. Address the envelope clearly, include a return address, and choose the right postage. For letters that carry weight — a cancellation, a formal complaint — many people opt for a tracked service such as Certified Mail (USPS) or Signed For / Recorded delivery (Royal Mail) so there's a record of sending and receipt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How should I address someone if I don't know their name?

Use "Dear Sir or Madam," in the UK or "To Whom It May Concern," in the US. If you can find the recipient's name with a quick check, though, a named greeting is always warmer and more effective.

### Is it rude to type a personal letter instead of handwriting it?

Not at all. Handwriting adds intimacy for close relationships, but a cleanly typed and printed letter is perfectly polite — and often preferable for formal or professional correspondence where legibility matters.

### What's the correct way to sign off a formal letter?

Match the sign-off to your greeting. UK: "Yours sincerely," (name known) or "Yours faithfully," (name unknown). US: "Sincerely," or "Best regards." Leave space for your signature and print your name below.

### Do I still need to follow letter etiquette for emails?

Many of the same principles carry over — a proper greeting, a clear structure, a courteous tone, and a fitting sign-off — but email is generally a touch less formal. A physical letter still holds the higher standard.
