# How to Write a Cover Letter That Gets Read

Learning how to write a cover letter that actually gets read is one of the highest-value skills in a job search. Recruiters skim — often in seconds — and most cover letters are indistinguishable from one another, especially now that so many are generated by AI and sound the same. A letter that's specific, confident, and clearly written for *this* role is what stops the skim and earns you the interview. Here's how to write one that stands out in 2026.

## Keep It Short and Focused

Surveys consistently find that most employers prefer a cover letter of half a page or less. Aim for three or four tight paragraphs on a single page. A recruiter deciding in seconds will thank you for getting to the point, and a shorter letter forces you to lead with your strongest material.

## Open With a Hook, Not a Formula

Skip "I am writing to apply for the [role] position I saw advertised." It's the most common opening line and the most forgettable. Instead, lead with something specific — a relevant result, a genuine reason you want *this* job at *this* company, or a short, concrete story. Make the first two lines impossible to have written for any other application.

## Tailor It to the Role and Company

The strongest cover letters are so specific they couldn't work anywhere else. Address the hiring manager by name where you can, name the role, and show you understand what the company actually does. Mirror a few keywords from the job description — many employers screen with applicant tracking systems — but weave them in naturally rather than stuffing them.

## Show Evidence, Not Adjectives

Don't call yourself "results-driven" — prove it. Quantify your achievements: "cut onboarding time by 30%," "grew the newsletter from 2,000 to 15,000 subscribers," "managed a £250k budget." Numbers are concrete, credible, and far more memorable than a list of adjectives.

## Tell a Short, Honest Story

Your cover letter is the one place to add the narrative a CV can't: why you care about this field, how your path led here, what genuinely draws you to this team. Keep it brief and keep it true — a real, specific story beats a polished but generic one.

## Sound Like a Person, Not a Template

Because so many letters now read as AI-generated boilerplate, an authentic human voice is a competitive advantage. Write in plain, confident language. Avoid clichés like "I am a hard worker" and defensive openers like "Although I don't have direct experience…". Present what you bring, plainly and without apology.

## Proofread Ruthlessly

A single typo in a cover letter can end an application. Read it twice, run a spellcheck, and ask someone you trust to look it over. Double-check the company name and the hiring manager's name — nothing sinks a letter faster than addressing it to the wrong firm.

## A Quick Cover Letter Structure

- **Opening:** a specific hook and the role you want.
- **Middle:** one or two paragraphs of evidence — quantified achievements matched to the job's needs.
- **Closing:** why this company, and a confident call to action ("I'd welcome the chance to discuss how I can contribute").
- **Sign-off:** "Sincerely" or "Yours sincerely," then your name.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a cover letter be?

Half a page to one page — three or four short paragraphs. Most employers prefer brevity, and recruiters often decide in seconds, so lead with your strongest, most specific material.

### How do I start a cover letter?

Skip the generic "I am writing to apply for…". Open with a hook: a relevant achievement, a concrete reason you want this specific role, or a short story. Address the hiring manager by name if you can find it.

### Should I use AI to write my cover letter?

Use it for brainstorming or tightening if you like, but don't send raw AI output. Many letters now sound identical because of it, and generic letters get ignored. Rewrite in your own voice with specific, verifiable details.

### How is a cover letter different from a CV or résumé?

Your CV lists what you've done; your cover letter explains why it matters for this role and shows your personality and motivation. It's the narrative and the pitch, not a repetition of your bullet points.
